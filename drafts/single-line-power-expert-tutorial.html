Design a data-center single-line in one sentence

Type “48 MW GB300 campus, Tier III, distributed 4-to-make-3 UPS, 2N to the rack,” press Design, and Drybulb's Power Expert sizes the whole utility-to-chip chain, draws a valid single-line, and writes the investor brief — in under a second.

Tools Walkthrough · Drybulb ≈ 9 min read · Updated June 21, 2026

A power single-line is the first artifact every data-center project argues over and the last one anybody enjoys drawing. It is where capacity, redundancy, and capital cost all become concrete — and where a sales conversation either earns trust or loses it. Drybulb's AI Factory Single-line tool now ships with a Power Expert: a natural-language front end that turns a one-sentence brief into a sized, electrically-valid topology you can edit on a canvas and roll straight into the cost model. This is a walkthrough of how to use it.

The Power Expert encodes the canonical hyperscale distribution chain described in SemiAnalysis's Datacenter Anatomy1 and the Uptime Institute tier definitions2. It is not a chatbot that improvises — it is a deterministic engineering generator with a language parser bolted to the front. Same sentence in, same single-line out, every time, and it will never hand you a diagram that fails its own electrical checks. We'll come back to why that matters for putting it in front of investors.

§ 01Open the tool

Sign in and go to Tools → AI Factory Single-line (/dashboard/tools/single-line). It opens on a worked default — the NVIDIA DGX SuperPOD reference shape, GB300 NVL72 racks — so you always have a valid starting point. Two strips sit above the canvas: the toolbar (Save, Load, undo/redo, AutoBuild, Clear) and, just beneath it, the Power Expert bar. That second strip is the one we want.

§ 02Describe your facility

Step · the prompt

Type a brief into the Power Expert field and press Design (or hit Enter). You can write the way you'd brief a colleague:

48 MW GB300 campus, Tier III, N+1 generation, distributed 4-to-make-3 UPS, 2N to the rack

You don't have to fill in everything. Anything you leave out takes a sourced default — distributed 4-to-make-3 UPS, 2N distribution, Tier III, PUE 1.2, GB300 racks. Three example chips under the field load instantly if you'd rather start from one and tweak. Here's the grammar the parser actually understands:

What the Power Expert reads

Capacity. Any “N MW.” Say “critical” or “IT” and it's read as IT load; say “facility,” “total,” or “grid” and it's treated as facility power and back-solved to IT through the PUE.

PUE. “PUE 1.25.” Defaults to 1.2.

Platform. GB300 (120 kW/rack), GB200 (132 kW), Vera Rubin / NVL144 (200 kW), or an explicit “N kW/rack.”

UPS redundancy. distributed / 4-to-make-3, 3-to-make-2, 2N, N+1, or catcher / N+2C.

Generation & distribution. N+1 or 2N generation; 2N or N to the rack. Generators sit on the 480 V site voltage by default — say “MV generators” to put them on the medium-voltage bus instead.

BESS. On by default at the interconnection (PCC); opt out with “no BESS” / “no battery.”

Tier & cooling. Tier I–IV; liquid, hybrid, or air. A name in quotes or “<Name> campus” becomes the design title.

§ 03Read what it built

Step · the design + the brief

The canvas redraws to your topology and a green toast confirms the headline — for the prompt above, designed 57.6 MW · 400 racks · 28,800 GPUs. A Design brief panel opens in the top-right corner with the numbers you'd put in front of a non-engineer: facility and IT megawatts, rack and GPU counts, the topology in one line, the Tier rationale, and a major-equipment schedule. Hit Copy to drop the whole brief (as Markdown) into a deck or email.

Read the single-line top to bottom and it matches the brief block for block. At the top, utility feeds two main transformers into a main-tie-main MV substation — MV SWBD-A and MV SWBD-B joined by a normally-open tie breaker, the secondary-selective scheme that lets you lose a transformer (or take one down for maintenance) without dropping the load.3 A BESS sits at the interconnection (the point of common coupling) for grid services and faster utility interconnection.5 Below the MV boards, the 4-to-make-3 UPS layer is transformer-plus-UPS blocks split across the two MV buses: any three of four carry the load, so each UPS runs near 75% — cheaper per megawatt than a fully duplicated 2N bank.1 Standby generators sit on the 480 V site-distribution voltage, paralleled on a generator switchboard that backs up the LV side.4 Finally, 2N distribution splits into A and B switchboards, then RPP row panels, and every rack is dual-corded — one breaker from an A panel, one from a B panel.

The diagram shows one representative power block; the schedule carries the full count. That's how engineers actually read a single-line — “typical pod, twenty total.”Why 48 MW doesn't draw 400 rack icons

§ 04Refine it on the canvas

Step · direct edit

The generated design is a normal editable graph, not a picture. Everything on the canvas is live:

Drag cards to lay them out. Click a card to open the inspector, where you can rename it, set a voltage or rating, add or remove feeder breakers, and — on a compute rack — apply an AI-rack spec that snaps the kW and auto-sizes the feeding circuit. Drag from a port to wire a new connection; the wire previews green when the connection is legal and red when it isn't. Click a wire to select it, then delete or re-route it. Every edit is undoable (Ctrl/⌘+Z), and a dev-time validator runs after each change so an illegal topology can't sneak in. If you wire something the engine won't accept — a loop, a reversed feed, a second circuit off a breaker already in use — it refuses the edit and tells you why instead of silently corrupting the graph.

§ 05Roll it up and share it

Step · cost model + library

The left rail keeps a running roll-up — rack count, GPUs, IT megawatts, and network-rack counts — that feeds the Drybulb Cost Model, so the topology you drew and the capital number you quote come from the same source. Name the design and click Save to keep it in your library; Load brings it back later. Iterating on a variant is just editing the prompt — change “distributed 4-to-make-3” to “catcher” and Design again to compare a reserve-UPS scheme against the distributed one.

§ 07Why it stays honest in front of investors

Three design choices make the Power Expert safe to demo, not just clever. First, it's deterministic: the same brief always produces the same single-line and the same schedule, because a sizing function — not an improvising model — does the engineering. Second, it self-validates: the generator builds the graph and runs the single-line engine's own checks before anything touches the canvas; if a design wouldn't pass, the tool declines rather than showing you a broken diagram. Third, it's sourced: every rating, pod size, and redundancy rule traces back to a knowledge base distilled from the SemiAnalysis electrical anatomy1 and the Uptime tiers2, with estimates labeled as estimates. You can hand the brief to a banker and defend every line.

What it is not is a substitute for a basis of design. The schedule is a fast, defensible first pass — use it to frame scale, compare redundancy schemes, and seed the cost model, then verify against the governing NVIDIA reference architecture and a real electrical engineer before anyone breaks ground.

Open the tool, type one sentence about the factory you're trying to build, and press Design. You'll have a single-line and a brief before you've finished describing it out loud.
