When the load trips the grid: the new global rules for data center ride-through, ramps and curtailment

Between July 2024 and July 2026, grid operators stopped treating data centers as passive load and started regulating them more like generators. What is now binding, what is still proposed, what was rejected, and what it means for the electrical design of an AI campus.

Drybulb · Analyst Note ≈ 19 min read · Status as of October 18, 2026
Key takeaways
  • Data center load lost on faults is growing. Fault-induced transfers to backup power in Northern Virginia grew from about 1,500 MW (July 2024) to about 3,800–3,900 MW (July 2026). Each began with a normally cleared fault; the sites' own UPS and transfer logic removed the load.1,2,3
  • A GPU campus is not a legacy IT load. Synchronous training swings tens of megawatts at 0.2–3 Hz, overlapping grid electromechanical modes, and dip-counting transfer logic plus constant-power rectifiers turn routine reclosing into a common-mode loss.4,5
  • Binding rules now exist in ERCOT, Dominion, AEP, SPP, Finland and Ireland (approval per law-firm reports). With the leading proposals, they converge on riding through six sags in 90 s, no dip counting, 90% power back in 0.5–3 s, a 150% current cap and ramps of roughly 10–30 MW/min.6,7,8,9,10
  • Curtailment arrives through the connection gate (Ireland, Alberta, Texas, SPP). FERC rejected PJM's curtail-first service on October 9, 2026.11,12,13
  • UPS firmware, transfer settings, rectifier current limits, power-factor control and BESS sizing are now grid-compliance items, not internal reliability choices.

At about 7:00 p.m. on July 10, 2024, a lightning arrestor failed on a 230 kV line in Dominion Energy's Northern Virginia zone. Auto-reclosing at both ends produced "6 successive system faults in an 82-second period," each lasting 42–66 ms, with load-area voltage sagging to 0.25–0.40 pu.1 No utility breaker disconnected a customer. Yet about 1,500 MW of "exclusively data center-type load" left the system, removed by the customers' own protection and controls.1 NERC traced it to transfer logic: "The typical number of voltage disturbances that trigger this scheme is three, and a typical time is one minute." About 1,260 MW dropped on the third dip "and did not return for hours."1 The data centers protected their IT load exactly as designed, and in doing so created a gigawatt-scale disturbance.

§ 1Two years of load-loss events

The pattern repeated. A February 17, 2025 single-line-to-ground 230 kV fault, cleared in about 5.9 cycles, produced four voltage depressions and about 1,800 MW of customer-initiated load reduction (CILR) according to NERC and PJM; PJM-side press reporting gave about 1,500 MW.3,14,15 Faults on May 3 and June 19, 2025 removed about 540 and 1,300 MW.14 Then settings changes showed what is possible: on April 18, 2026, a similar fault in the same area caused "only 500 MW of sustained data center load loss," after operators "lengthened the time duration required for measuring voltage dips to be 'counted.'"16

Three months later came the largest event yet. On July 22, 2026, a 230 kV fault led to roughly 3,800 MW transferring to backup; reporting ACE reached +3,928 MW and frequency 60.092 Hz, with "no relay misoperations or stuck breakers."2 PJM's later deck revises the total to about 3,900 MW: about 2,900 MW in a first wave, then "roughly ~1,000 MW of CILR a minute later" as voltage peaked near 1.138 pu.3 NERC called it a "nearly 4 GW customer-initiated load reduction… demonstrating that the risk is increasing, not abating."16

Texas and Ireland have their own records. ERCOT counted 26 trip events since 2023 (as of December 2025);17 NERC's draft cites 32 events of 100 MW or more through April 2026, mostly crypto mining, and notes ERCOT "has not observed any VRT events at a traditional data center."14 Ireland has had five faults since 2022 with data center demand drops of 74 to 480 MW (16–52% of data center demand).18

Ireland shows what this does to a small system. The TSOs treat 900 MW as the largest manageable load rejection and assume 90% of data center demand can drop on a credible fault. In late June 2026 data center demand neared 1,000 MW, the limit "was reached," and interconnector exports were cut.18 EirGrid also paused raising its non-synchronous penetration limit from 75% to 80% because of the ride-through issue.9 Data center behavior now caps renewable operation. No comparable event has been documented in Asia-Pacific, Latin America or the Middle East; regulators there cite Virginia and Ireland as precedent.19

§ 2The physics: why a GPU campus is not a legacy IT load

To the grid, a traditional data center is a large, flat load that occasionally disappears onto its generators. An AI training campus differs in four ways, and each rule below targets one of them.

The load is synchronized. Training alternates compute and communication phases across thousands of GPUs at once. Microsoft, OpenAI and NVIDIA researchers report swings of "tens of megawatts" in one data center and "tens or hundreds of megawatts" at scale, with spectral energy at 0.2–3 Hz, overlapping inter-area and plant modes and approaching turbine torsional modes from about 7 Hz.4 NERC warns that cycling in the "0.1–2 Hz" electromechanical range can cause "widespread forced oscillations," and that oscillations have already occurred at a crypto facility in ERCOT and a data center in Dominion.5

The protection logic is common-mode. Dip counting protects servers from a sick utility. But multi-shot reclosing on a permanent fault is the grid working as designed, and when every site on a network applies the same three-dips rule, they all leave together. NERC: "all computational loads on a circuit could see the same disturbance and take the same action."16 Some fleets compound it: for DRUPS, retransfer to the grid "must be done manually."1

The front end holds power constant. Double-conversion UPS units hold power constant through a dip, which can deepen it, and unstandardized reconnection risks "flapping."20 At constant power, current scales as 1/V, so full power at 0.67 pu means 150% current; ERCOT observed 150–200% in real events.21

Shedding load pushes voltage and frequency up. In July 2024 voltage rose to 1.07 pu and operators removed capacitor banks;1 in July 2026 the overvoltage from the first wave tripped the second.3 One ERCOT voltage event "became a frequency control event" at 60.235 Hz for over 10 minutes.22 High-voltage ride-through is now a design case.

The grid did not fail. Sites tripped on normally cleared faults that reclosing practice creates on purpose. — The common thread of every documented event

§ 3Ride-through and post-fault recovery: the envelope is converging

North America

NERC moved from study to near-mandate in about 20 months. Its May 4, 2026 Level 3 alert is not a standard, but reporting was mandatory. It asks for PERC1-class models with "UPS settings and configurations" and "expected maximum ramp rate (MW/min) up and down," a ±10% voltage step test at commissioning, dynamic fault recorders, and compliance with operator instructions.23 On July 16, 2026 FERC ordered NERC to file computational-load standards and registry criteria by December 31, 2026, with penalties of up to $1,584,648 per violation per day once registered.24 Phase 1 "will not address the minimum voltage or frequency ride-through"; that is expected in a 2027 standard.2 The registration threshold is unsettled: 20 MW at 60 kV in NERC's April proposal, 50 MW at 100 kV in a later draft known only from a PJM slide.2,25 NERC's draft ride-through white paper recommends ERCOT's curve as a national floor, extended to full reclosing sequences and cooling load, with 1 s recovery "achievable and appropriate" in strong systems and up to 10 s in weak ones.14

ERCOT's NOGRR 282 is the most complete adopted load ride-through rule anywhere. Approved by the PUCT on July 9, 2026 and effective August 1, 2026, it covers sites of 75 MW or more that are at least half power-electronic computational load (scope per the companion NPRR 1308), with sites energized or contracted by November 14, 2025 exempt.6,21,26 Beyond the envelope in Fig. 2 and Table 1, it lets sites trip for overvoltage above 1.08 pu lasting 5 s only until January 1, 2028, requires continued consumption at 0.5–0.8 pu after that date (proportional reduction allowed) or a compliant transfer scheme, lets cooling trip only below 0.35 pu, and sets a 90/90/180-day root-cause and correction cycle with disconnection possible under imminent risk.6 Jurisdictional challenges were left unaddressed.27

PJM has no binding RTO-wide load requirement yet. Its September 15, 2026 proposal, Manual 14B Attachment D-2, largely transplants ERCOT for loads of 50 MW or more at 60 kV and above, starting with projects entering study on January 16, 2027.28 Dominion's Attachment 8 already binds: data centers above 50 MW "must demonstrate the capability to ride through at least six automatic reclosing attempts that last 50–70 milliseconds each."7 AEP's Rev 7 requires only "two consecutive voltage excursions" outside 0.92–1.05 pu.29 Southern Company has published no load ride-through or ramp requirements that this research could find; its practice is known only through CAISO's survey.10 CAISO's June 15, 2026 straw proposal is the most demanding US draft on sag duration (6 s below 0.9 pu) but the slowest on recovery (3 s), with a FERC filing targeted for November 16, 2026.10,30 SPP's HILL rules, effective January 15, 2026, add telemetry, remote disconnect, a customer-funded PMU and ride-through guidelines for loads of 50 MW or more above 69 kV.31,32

Europe and Australia: faster clocks

Ireland's MPID345 requires demand to stay connected through faults and return to "at least 90% of its prefault Active Power Demand within 500 ms," with ±1 Hz/s RoCoF withstand. Sites may ride on backup during the dip provided grid draw returns in time.9 Industry argued no "off the shelf" solution exists; the CRU's adviser found "no feasible, practical, or deliverable technical alternative."9,33 Law-firm reports say the CRU approved it on September 22, 2026, effective five weeks later; the decision itself was not reviewed.34 The proposed 24-month derogation caps non-compliant demand in MVA, a direct commercial lever on existing sites.18

Fingrid's requirements (June 12, 2026; new demand of 30 MW or more) demand survival of eight 100 ms three-phase faults in 90 s and 90% recovery within 1 s, using storage or resistors if needed.8 (NERC's draft lists Fingrid at 0.5 s;14 Fingrid's text says 1 s.) The EU Demand Connection Code has no load ride-through clause and its revision is stalled, so ENTSO-E's non-binding December 2025 template asks TSOs to set recovery of 80–100% within 0.5–5 s and RoCoF withstand up to ±4 Hz/s over 0.25 s.35,36 Belgium has consulted on rules above 75 MW; Great Britain has none adopted.37,38

Australia's ERC0394 draft (final due October 29, 2026) would require 500 ms recovery under the automatic standard, cap consumption at 110% during undervoltage, extend overvoltage ride-through to 130% and bar trips "based solely on the number of faults." The AEMC concedes "some UPS models… cannot withstand 130% overvoltages" but argues recovery can usually be fixed "through control system changes rather than hardware changes," a claim Irish industry disputes.19,39

Elsewhere, obligations are soft. Saudi Arabia asks loads to mitigate "transient voltage dips" qualitatively;40 Mexico requires load centers to ride ±10% voltage for 20 minutes, per a vendor summary;41 Chile instead requires a large client to study the grid impact of its own sudden trip and add redundancy if needed;42 India has no load ride-through code;43 and Singapore puts dip mitigation on the customer.44

§ 4Ramp rates and oscillation limits

Ride-through rules govern what a site does when the grid misbehaves. Ramp and oscillation rules govern what the site does to the grid, and here the record is thinner and often second-hand.

Where ramp limits exist they cluster at 10–30 MW/min (Table 2). SPP's adopted cap is 20 MW/min;32 CAISO proposes 20 MW/min over a rolling 10 minutes;10 Fingrid requires a selectable 5–50 MW/min limiter and a 50 MW step cap.8 Dominion's often-quoted ~10 MW/min is a classification trigger for "non-conforming" load, not a cap.45 ERCOT has no adopted MW/min limit that this research found, despite a 20 MW/min entry in CAISO's survey; it manages swings through Regulation, which large-load swings exceeded in 255 five-minute intervals in the year to February 2024.10,22

Numeric oscillation limits are rarer. Dominion requires minimal injection in four bands from 0.01 to 55 Hz;7 Fingrid sets per-band limits from 0.01 to 45 Hz, under which a 300 MW site may cause at most 12 MW peak oscillation at 1 Hz.8 NERC has published no number.5 An academic review reports a 23 Hz, 50 MW peak-to-peak oscillation at an ERCOT large load in October 2024, unconfirmed by any primary source.46 IEEE P4200, authorized June 4, 2026, will cover "oscillation and ramp rate management, power quality" for data centers, but is years from publication.47

§ 5Curtailment, demand response and flexible connection

Mandatory curtailment of data centers is real but narrow, and it is almost always a condition of connection rather than an operating rule.

  • Ireland (adopted December 12, 2025): sites of 10 MVA or more must bring dispatchable generation or storage matching their import capacity and cannot "ramp up to its full MIC" until it is delivered. Compliant sites are exempt from Mandatory Demand Curtailment; others can be cut on 60 minutes' notice.11
  • Alberta (in force June 9, 2026): bridged data centers "reduce demand before any other market participant."12
  • Texas SB 6 (signed June 21, 2025): loads of 75 MW or more need curtailment equipment installed before interconnection, but ERCOT listed the protocol as "Not Started" in May 2026 and no interface specification exists.49
  • SPP CHILLS (accepted June 5, 2026): curtailable transmission service "for up to seven years" (an earlier description said five).50,51
  • Australia: South Australia can direct sites of 100 MW or more to vary load "by up to 100%"; NSW proposes 25% cuts for up to two hours for sites of 15 MW or more, and "diesel generators are not to be used for load flexibility."52
  • Defaults in older codes: Saudi Arabia can place 100% of a user's load on under-frequency relays;40 Chile mandates participation in automatic load-shedding pools;42 Dominion requires UFLS participation.7

PJM's attempt failed. The Board's January 2026 "connect and manage" plan, refiled as the Interim Resource Adequacy Service, would have curtailed new large loads "prior to the deployment of Pre-Emergency Load Management."53,54 On October 9, 2026 FERC rejected it because it would "dictate which retail customers taking retail service will be curtailed," keeping only the 50 MW definition and a registry.13 The pattern is jurisdictional: where one authority controls both wholesale and retail service, curtail-first mandates have held; under the US federal/state split they have not. Open proposals include Great Britain's "mandatory minimum level of demand flexibility,"55 Israel's peak disconnection power (alongside an adopted 140-day freeze on applications of 8 MW or more),56 and AEMO's direction powers.48

Two phenomena — do not conflate them

Planned flexibility is an asset. Duke's Rethinking Load Growth estimated 76 / 98 / 126 GW of new load could connect at 0.25 / 0.5 / 1.0% annual curtailment,57 or 85 / 177 / 366 hours a year.58 EPRI's DCFlex test delivered "load reductions of up to 40%… with subminute response times."59 Google's I&M contract includes "a PJM Emergency qualifying demand-response program," with confidential quantities.60

Unplanned sub-second CILR is a liability. Operators want to buy the first and are legislating against the second.

§ 6Power quality: old limits, newly enforced

Harmonic, flicker and power-factor limits mostly predate AI. What changed is enforcement at the point of interconnection. NERC tells utilities that because IEEE 519-2022 is not mandatory they "should include harmonic limits in their interconnection" requirements, asks for spectra to the 100th order, and warns of flicker even where interharmonic limits are met.5 Dominion sets Pst ≤ 0.8 and Plt ≤ 0.6, limits interharmonics to 25% of IEEE 519 values and requires POI PQ meters with 90 days of retention;45 AEP requires IEEE 519-2022 and IEEE 1453-2022.29

Power-factor rules vary by jurisdiction and, in Chile, tighten with voltage: 0.95 lagging or leading at Dominion;7 0.95 lagging at AEP;29 0.9 equivalent under the EU code;35 0.97 lagging to unity in Mexico from April 8, 2026 (vendor summary);41 0.98 at 100 kV and above in Chile, which also halves harmonic limits and caps voltage THD at 3% above 110 kV;42 95% lagging with 1.5% THD in Saudi Arabia.40 An Indian study reports unmitigated data center THD of 5–15%, below 5% with 12-pulse or active-front-end rectifiers.43 Two consequences matter for AI campuses: leading power factor at light load can breach two-sided limits, and switched capacitors can cause "high overvoltage" when load has not yet recovered after a fault, as Fingrid warns.8 In July 2026 it was the load loss itself that drove voltage up and tripped the second wave; Dominion corrected the overvoltage by tripping capacitor banks.2,3

§ 7Commercial terms and access rationing

Large-load tariffs enforce financial commitment, not behavior: 85% minimum demand at AEP Ohio, whose tariff also requires behind-the-meter generation to be matched by "equipment that can instantly curtail load";61 85% of delivery and 60% of generation demand under Dominion's GS-5 from 2027;62 contracts of up to 15 years with minimum bills in Georgia;63 80% at Evergy Kansas;64 and 85%/75% in Malaysia.65 Texas's new interconnection rule reportedly requires security of $50,000/MW.66 Across much of Asia and Latin America the lever is access itself: Taiwan's suspension of new data centers above 5 MW in the north,67 Korea's grid assessment for loads of 10 MW or more,68 and Brazil's first competitive round, which awarded 228 MW against 451 MW of qualified demand.69

§ 9What this means for owners and engineers

The data center's internal reliability logic now counts as a system-level fault in its own right. In Drybulb's view, eight changes belong in every large-load basis of design.

  1. Ban dip-counting transfer logic. Specify ride-through of at least six sags or swells in 90 s (eight 100 ms faults where Finland applies) and sag-detection timers of 80–120 ms or more.6,7,8 Virginia's April 2026 result shows settings changes on existing plant work.16
  2. Write the recovery clock into the UPS specification. Ask vendors for tested recovery-to-90% times against the 0.5–3 s range in Fig. 3, current limiting at or below 150%, and voltage-proportional power reduction below about 0.8 pu. A transfer to generators with delayed retransfer to the grid no longer qualifies as ride-through where these rules apply.
  3. Design for high-voltage ride-through. Cover 1.2 pu for 1–2 s,6,10 and coordinate capacitor and filter switching with post-fault recovery.
  4. Extend ride-through to mechanical plant. ERCOT and PJM let cooling trip only below 0.35 pu, so chiller, pump and fan drives need ride-through-capable VFDs or UPS support.6,28
  5. Size BESS for dynamic range, not just backup minutes. Plan for a 10–30 MW/min ramp limiter, a step limit and spectral limits in the 0.01–2 Hz and 5–55 Hz bands.7,8,10 A GPU power floor's energy penalty is IT energy that PUE will not show; carry it and the BESS capital explicitly in the Cost Model alongside the PUE view.
  6. Lay out the utility interface for the new topology rules. Dominion bars load transfer between transmission sources and networked configurations, and requires delta HV windings, so redundancy must sit inside the radial customer system.7 The Single-line tool is a quick way to compare those topologies before the utility study.
  7. Treat on-site generation as a grid-facing ramp. Dominion forbids net POI load from rising when it trips; AEP Ohio requires matching instant curtailment; NERC flags co-located generation for torsional-interaction risk.5,7,61
  8. Instrument the POI and make curtailment a feature. Budget for PQ meters, a PMU, dynamic fault recorders, telemetry and a curtailment interface, plus commissioning tests with as-built UPS settings in the utility's model.23,32,45,49

Retrofit exposure varies. ERCOT exempts sites energized by November 14, 2025;6 PJM's draft would reach legacy sites by January 16, 2028;28 Ireland covers existing sites with time-limited derogations;18 the AEMC draft and Fingrid are not retrospective.8,19 PJM notes that "all observed Customer Initiated Load Reduction (CILR) events have involved already operational facilities."3 A design specified now to the strictest common denominator, riding through six to eight shots in 90 s, recovering 90% in 0.5 s, holding current under 150%, tolerating 1.2 pu for 2 s and ramping at about 20 MW/min, should clear nearly any connection agreement now in draft. Retrofitting to it later will generally cost more.

§ 10What to watch next

  • October 29, 2026: AEMC final determination on ERC0394.39
  • Late October 2026: Ireland's MPID345 takes effect, five weeks after the reported approval.34
  • Around November 16, 2026: CAISO's FERC filing and other RTO responses to the June show-cause orders.30
  • December 31, 2026: NERC's standards and registry filing, settling the size and voltage threshold.24
  • January 16, 2027: proposed start of PJM's D-2 requirements for new projects.28
  • Early 2027: Fingrid's KJV2026, extending tiered requirements to demand from 1 MW.79
  • 2027 and beyond: NERC's ride-through standard,2 ERCOT's January 1, 2028 tightening,6 and IEEE P4200.47

Oscillation limits for AI training remain the least settled frontier. Dominion and Fingrid have numbers; NERC and CAISO do not yet. Owners of gigawatt-scale training campuses should expect forbidden-band spectral limits in interconnection agreements before any standard requires them.
